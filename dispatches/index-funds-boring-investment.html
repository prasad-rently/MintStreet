Index Funds: The Boring Investment That Keeps Winning

An investment strategy built entirely around not trying to be clever. Decades of evidence say that's exactly why it keeps winning.

6 min read · Supplementary dispatch

Most of what makes a good investing story — a bold call, a contrarian bet, a fortune made by seeing what everyone else missed — is completely absent from an index fund. It doesn't try to pick winners. It just buys a small piece of every company in a market index, like the Nifty 50 or the S&P 500, in the same proportion as the index itself. No manager decides what to buy or sell. That's the entire strategy, and it's precisely the point.

An idea that started as a failure

The intellectual seed goes back further than you'd expect: in 1951, a Princeton undergraduate named John Bogle wrote his senior thesis arguing that the fees funds charged were a serious, underappreciated drag on what investors actually kept. Twenty-five years later, in 1976, Bogle put that thesis into practice and launched what he called the First Index Investment Trust — the first index fund built for ordinary individual investors, not just large institutions.

It flopped. Bogle hoped to raise $150 million; the launch pulled in just over $11 million, which he later called "an abject failure." Critics mocked it as "Bogle's Folly" and dismissed the whole idea as un-American — deliberately settling for average, when the entire industry was selling the promise of beating average. That fund is now known as the Vanguard 500 Index Fund, and it alone holds well over a trillion dollars today.

The evidence that proved the mockery wrong

The case for index funds isn't a matter of opinion anymore — it's one of the most heavily studied questions in finance, tracked for decades by scorecards like S&P's SPIVA reports, which compare actively managed funds against the plain index they're trying to beat. The results are strikingly consistent: over any given single year, most active fund managers can beat their benchmark. Stretch the window to 15 or 20 years, and roughly 88–93% of actively managed U.S. large-cap funds have underperformed the S&P 500. Worse for the case of picking a "good" manager: outperformance rarely repeats. A fund that beats the market for five years is not meaningfully more likely to keep doing it than one picked at random.

None of this means professional fund managers are unskilled. It means beating a market made up of millions of other participants — many of them other skilled professionals — is extraordinarily hard to do consistently, and every attempt costs something: research, trading, and a management fee, charged whether the bet works or not. An index fund skips all of that by not trying to win the game, and simply avoids most of its cost.

Why a "small" fee difference is actually enormous

India's own first index fund, UTI's Nifty index fund, launched in 2000 and opened to investors in 2002. Its direct-plan expense ratio today runs around 0.17% a year — a small fraction of what many actively managed equity funds charge. That gap looks trivial year to year. It isn't, once you remember what Module 1 and the SIP Calculator already showed you about compounding: a fee is a return you never get to compound. Shave even one or two percentage points off your annual return, every single year, for twenty or thirty years, and the gap between the two outcomes stops looking like a rounding error and starts looking like a meaningfully different retirement.

See it for yourself: try the same monthly amount and time horizon in the SIP Calculator at two different assumed return rates a couple of points apart — the gap in the maturity value is the fee, compounding against you instead of for you.

What it doesn't do

An index fund is not a shortcut around risk. When the market it tracks falls, it falls right along with it — there's no manager to duck out of a bad sector or raise cash before a downturn. It will also never beat the market, by design; it is the market, minus a sliver of fees. What it trades away is the chance at spectacular outperformance. What it trades in return is a very high probability of not badly lagging the market either — which, per the evidence above, is what happens to most people who try to beat it.

It's also, in its own quiet way, instant diversification — a single purchase spread across dozens or hundreds of companies at once, the same principle the Diversification Simulator on the Practice Sheets page demonstrates directly.

Boring, in other words, isn't a criticism here. It's the entire mechanism.
