The Harshad Mehta Scam and the Birth of SEBI

In 1992, one broker turned a routine piece of interbank paperwork into a ₹4,000 crore fraud — and became the loudest possible argument for giving India's market regulator real teeth.

6 min read · Supplementary dispatch

In April 1992, the Sensex was near an all-time high, and it felt to plenty of first-time investors like an honest bull run finally rewarding them for showing up. By August, it had fallen more than 40%. The story of what actually happened in between doesn't start on a trading floor — it starts in the unglamorous, entirely routine corner of the money market where banks quietly lend to each other.

A paperwork shortcut, not a loophole

Banks constantly need to manage short-term cash. One safe, boring way they do this is by temporarily trading government securities with each other — one bank sells, agreeing to buy back shortly after, effectively borrowing cash against very safe collateral. Physically moving bond certificates for every one of these short-term deals was impractical, so banks used a shortcut instead: a Bank Receipt (BR), a signed document essentially saying "we are holding these government securities on your behalf." It was meant to be paperwork, not a product — a stand-in for a real, existing asset.

Harshad Mehta, a stockbroker who positioned himself as a middleman in these interbank deals, found the gap in that system: get a bank to issue a Bank Receipt with no actual government securities behind it at all. Banks handed over real cash believing they were buying real, safe securities. Mehta took that cash — not paperwork, actual money — and poured it straight into the stock market, building enormous, concentrated positions in a handful of favored stocks and driving their prices up sharply as he bought.

The unwind

The scheme worked for as long as nobody looked too closely at what those Bank Receipts were actually backed by. That changed in April 1992, when journalist Sucheta Dalal's reporting exposed that receipts held by banks — including State Bank of India and National Housing Bank — corresponded to securities that simply didn't exist. Roughly ₹4,000 crore had moved from the banking system into the market this way. Once the fraud became public, the banks wanted their money back, the artificially inflated stocks had no real support underneath them, and the Sensex gave back its gains and then some — a more than 40% fall between April and August 1992.

Where SEBI actually comes in

It's tempting to tell this as "the scam created SEBI." The real timeline is more interesting. SEBI had already existed since 1988, but purely as an advisory body — it could observe the market and make recommendations, but had no legal power to investigate anyone or enforce a single rule. The SEBI Act, which finally gave it real statutory power, was passed in January 1992 — just months before the scam broke, not after.

So the Harshad Mehta scam didn't birth SEBI from nothing. What it did was land almost immediately after SEBI got real teeth, as the most visible possible demonstration of exactly why those teeth were necessary. A market regulator that could only write reports had just been proven useless against a fraud of this scale — and that made the case for statutory power impossible to argue with, accelerating SEBI's build-out from a paper watchdog into the regulator that actually investigates, penalizes, and sets binding rules today.

Full circle: the reforms that followed directly targeted the loopholes this scam exploited — NSE's 1994 launch as a transparent, electronic exchange replaced the kind of opaque broker-to-broker dealing Mehta relied on, and dematerialized (demat) shares with electronic settlement removed the paper-certificate vulnerabilities entirely.

It's a useful case study for something Module 2 only had room to mention in passing: the same openness and trust that make a stock market work are exactly what a determined fraud tries to exploit. The lesson isn't that markets are untrustworthy — it's that trust in a market is only as good as the enforcement standing behind it, which is precisely what SEBI exists to be now.
