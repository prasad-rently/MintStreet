Gold as an Asset Class: Why Indians Never Stopped Buying It

Indian households, together, hold more gold than most of the world's central banks combined. That's not superstition — it's a rational answer to a question every asset on this site eventually has to face.

6 min read · Supplementary dispatch

Here's a number worth sitting with: Indian households, including what's held in temples, own an estimated 25,000 tonnes of gold — worth roughly $2.4 trillion. The Reserve Bank of India's own official reserves, by comparison, are a little over 800 tonnes. Ordinary Indian households, collectively, hold more gold than their own central bank, and more than most countries on Earth hold in their national vaults.

The question every asset has to answer

Every asset this site has covered so far rests on somebody's promise. A rupee note is a promise from the RBI. A bond is a promise from whoever issued it to pay you back. A share is a claim on a company that has to keep existing and keep being run honestly to be worth anything. Gold is the one major asset that doesn't ask you to trust anyone. No government, company, or institution has to keep a commitment for a gold coin to still be gold a hundred years from now.

In a country that has lived through shifting rulers, a currency still young by the standards of the rupee's modern form, and a formal banking system that only reached most of the population within living memory, "doesn't need anyone's promise" was never an abstract virtue — it was a practical one. Gold is portable, divisible into whatever amount you need, and recognized as valuable everywhere, which made it a natural store of value for a large, geographically spread, largely agrarian population long before demat accounts or bank branches reached most villages. That same practicality is why gold shows up at weddings, gets passed down between generations, and is bought deliberately around festivals like Dhanteras and Akshaya Tritiya — it was, for most of Indian history, simply the most reliable way an ordinary family could hold savings.

What gold actually is, financially

Strip away the sentiment and gold has a specific, unusual profile as an asset. It pays no dividend and no interest — unlike a share or a bond, gold sitting in a locker generates zero income; whatever return you get comes entirely from its price changing. Over long periods, it has held up reasonably well against inflation, broadly preserving purchasing power even as currencies lose it (the exact concern Module 1's Shrinking Rupee calculator puts a number on). And it tends to have a low correlation with stocks — it doesn't reliably move in the same direction as the equity market, which is precisely why the Diversification Simulator on the Practice Sheets page includes it as one of its four asset buckets: not because gold is a better investment than equities, but because it often zigs when equities zag.

When the government tried to stop the buying

Gold's grip on Indian savings has been strong enough that the state once tried to break it directly. After the 1962 war with China strained the country's foreign reserves, the Gold Control Act banned private ownership of gold bullion outright, forced banks to recall existing gold loans, and banned forward trading in gold. It's a striking episode: a government deciding that a culturally central asset was enough of an economic problem to legislate against.

It didn't particularly work — smuggling filled the gap the law created — and the Act was finally repealed on June 6, 1990, as India opened gold imports back up ahead of the broader economic liberalization that followed in 1991. The demand the law had tried to suppress simply picked back up once it was legal again.

Owning gold without owning gold

Physical gold has real costs that don't show up on the price tag: making charges on jewelry, the risk and expense of storing it safely, and questions of purity when it's time to sell — a hidden drag not unlike the expense ratios covered in the index funds dispatch. In 2015, the government launched Sovereign Gold Bonds specifically to offer an alternative: a bond that tracks the gold price and additionally pays about 2.5% interest a year, with no locker, no making charges, and no purity risk — part of a deliberate push to reduce the enormous import bill that India's physical gold appetite adds to its trade deficit each year. Gold ETFs offer a similar paper alternative through a regular brokerage account.

And because so much of the country already owns real, physical gold, an entire lending industry exists around it: gold loans, where jewelry serves as instant collateral for a quick loan with none of the paperwork a personal loan usually requires. It's a use case no mutual fund statement or SIP can offer — collateral you can literally carry to a lender the same afternoon.

None of this makes gold better or worse than the stocks, SIPs, and index funds covered elsewhere on this site. It answers a different question: what do you own that doesn't depend on any other institution keeping its word? For a very long stretch of Indian history, gold was the only honest answer to that question — and enough habit, trust, and genuine diversification value survives that it never really stopped being one.
