What a Candlestick Actually Tells You

Four numbers, one glance. How an 18th-century rice trader's shorthand became the default way the world reads a price chart.

5 min read · Supplementary dispatch

In the early 1700s, merchants at the Dōjima Rice Exchange in Osaka were doing something no market in the world had done in quite this way before: trading standardized contracts for rice that hadn't been harvested yet. It's widely considered the first organized futures market — and like every futures market since, it created a new problem. If you're betting on a price that will only be settled months from now, you need a way to track how that price is behaving right now, at a glance, day after day.

Japanese folklore credits one trader in particular with solving that problem with real flair: Munehisa Homma, a rice merchant from Sakata who became so successful trading at Dōjima that he was later nicknamed "the god of the markets." He's often credited as the father of candlestick charting, and he did write one of the earliest known texts touching on the psychology of trading, in 1755. Worth saying plainly, though: the exact candlestick shape used today is documented more clearly in Japanese sources from a century later, the Meiji era — so while the rice-market culture Homma came from is real and well documented, his personal authorship of the candlestick as we draw it now sits somewhere between history and legend. Markets, it turns out, have always loved a good origin story.

Four numbers, packed into one shape

Whatever its exact origin, the candlestick survived because it's a genuinely efficient piece of design. Every candle represents one time period — a day, an hour, a minute, whatever the chart is set to — and encodes exactly four numbers from that period:

Open
The price at the start of the period.
Close
The price at the end of the period.
High
The highest price reached at any point during the period.
Low
The lowest price reached at any point during the period.

Traders call this set "OHLC" for short. The thick rectangle in the middle of a candle — its body — spans the distance between the open and the close. The thin lines poking out above and below it — the wicks, or shadows — mark how far the price reached beyond that range before settling back down, up to the high and down to the low.

Why bother, when a line chart is simpler?

The SIP calculator and the MINT paper-trading sheet on the Practice Sheets page both draw a simple line — one point per day, connected. That's honest, and it's enough to see a trend. But a line only tells you where a period ended. It throws away everything that happened in between.

A candlestick keeps that texture. A long wick with a small body says the price tried to move hard in one direction and got pulled back — buyers and sellers fighting to a rough draw. A long body with barely any wick says one side won decisively, start to finish, no real resistance. None of that predicts what happens tomorrow. It just describes today, in much more detail than a single closing number ever could.

A word of caution

A large corner of trading education is built around naming candlestick shapes — "hammer," "doji," "engulfing pattern" — and implying that spotting one tells you what price will do next. Treat that the way this site treats every neat, confident story about markets: skeptically. A candlestick is a compressed description of something that has already happened. It's genuinely useful vocabulary for talking about price action with precision. It is not a crystal ball, and no shape on a chart removes the uncertainty that the rest of this site — from bubbles to 2008 — keeps coming back to.
