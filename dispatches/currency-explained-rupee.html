Currency Explained: Why the Rupee Moves

In 1991, India pledged 67 tonnes of its gold reserves just to keep paying its import bills. What that crisis revealed about why an exchange rate moves at all, on any ordinary day.

6 min read · Supplementary dispatch

By June 1991, India's foreign exchange reserves had fallen so low they covered barely three weeks of essential imports. The country was days away from being unable to pay for oil and other necessities it couldn't produce itself. To secure emergency funding, the government did something that still sounds startling: it physically pledged 67 tonnes of India's gold reserves as collateral — 47 tonnes airlifted to the Bank of England, 20 tonnes to a Swiss bank — to unlock loans that kept the country solvent. Days later, on July 1 and again on July 3, the government devalued the rupee against major currencies in two rapid moves, a combined shift of roughly a fifth in its value. That crisis is the direct reason the Gold Control Act had already been repealed the year before, and the reason the broader economic liberalization of 1991 happened at all.

What actually moves an exchange rate

A rupee's value as currency — what it can buy inside India — is a promise from the RBI, as the RBI dispatch described. Its exchange rate against other currencies is a completely different question, decided in the foreign exchange market: the largest, most liquid market in the world, where currencies are bought and sold against each other constantly. Like any market, its price moves on supply and demand — it's just that what's being bought and sold is money itself.

The forces behind that supply and demand

Trade flows
When India imports more than it exports, Indian importers must sell rupees and buy dollars to pay foreign sellers — extra dollar demand weakens the rupee. Exporters do the reverse, which is why a weaker rupee is actually good news for export-heavy businesses even as it makes imported goods costlier at home.
Capital flows
Recall the 2008 dispatch: when Foreign Institutional Investors pour money into Indian stocks and bonds, they're buying rupees to do it, which supports the currency. When they pull money out — exactly what happened after Lehman collapsed — they sell rupees for dollars, and the rupee weakens.
Interest rate differentials
When Indian interest rates sit meaningfully above rates elsewhere, foreign capital is drawn toward rupee-denominated assets chasing that extra yield, which tends to support the currency. Narrow that gap — or have the U.S. Federal Reserve raise rates faster — and capital can just as easily flow the other way.
Global dollar strength
Sometimes it isn't about India at all. When the dollar strengthens broadly against many currencies at once, most emerging-market currencies weaken right along with the rupee, purely as a side effect.

India's actual policy: a managed float

India doesn't let the rupee float completely freely, and it doesn't fix it at a set rate either — it runs what's called a managed float. Day to day, the market sets the rate. But the RBI keeps a large stockpile of foreign currency reserves specifically so it can step in — buying or selling dollars — to smooth out unusually sharp, fast moves. The goal isn't to hold the rupee at any particular level or push it in a preferred direction; it's to stop the transition from being disorderly, the same crisis-prevention instinct behind the RBI dispatch's description of it as regulator and stabilizer.

Depreciation vs. devaluation: today's day-to-day rupee moves are depreciation (or appreciation) — the ordinary drift of a market-set rate. 1991 was a devaluation: a deliberate, one-time government reset of an officially administered rate, back when the rupee wasn't market-determined the way it mostly is now.

Why any of this reaches an ordinary household

A weaker rupee makes imports costlier — crude oil most of all, since India imports the large majority of what it uses, which feeds straight into the inflation Module 1 already covered. It also makes an overseas holiday or a foreign university tuition bill more expensive in rupee terms. On the other side, it's a genuine tailwind for exporters and anyone earning income in dollars, since each dollar converts into more rupees than before.

The number on a currency-converter screen looks like a single fact. It's really the sum of trade bills being settled, foreign investors moving money in and out, interest rate decisions taken an ocean away, and the RBI quietly leaning against whatever's moving too fast — all landing, at that exact moment, on one exchange rate.
