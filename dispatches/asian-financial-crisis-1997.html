The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis: When Tigers Stumbled

Four currencies collapsed in four months. Not by coincidence — by a shared vulnerability that turned one country's problem into a region's.

7 min read · Supplementary dispatch

Through the 1980s and into the 1990s, Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, and Malaysia were held up as the "Asian Tigers" — export-driven growth stories pulling in huge amounts of foreign capital and admiration in roughly equal measure. Then, across four months in 1997, their currencies fell one after another like dominoes.

The trigger

Thailand's baht had effectively been pegged to the U.S. dollar for years. Defending that peg against sustained speculative pressure drained the Bank of Thailand's reserves, and on July 2, 1997, it gave up and let the baht float freely. The currency fell 15–20% almost immediately, with much further declines to come. It's the single date most historians point to as the crisis's start.

Contagion, on a calendar

What happened next reads like a timeline of falling dominoes. The Philippines floated the peso on July 12. Malaysia abandoned its own pegged rate on July 14. Indonesia floated the rupiah on August 14. By October, South Korea's won was in full crisis too. Four major currencies, four collapses, inside four months.

Why it spread so fast

The shared vulnerability was structural, not superstition. These economies had all borrowed heavily in foreign currency — mostly dollars — while earning their actual revenue in local currency, a dangerous mismatch. A lot of that borrowed capital had also flowed into over-leveraged banks and speculative real estate rather than genuinely productive investment, propped up by cozy, under-regulated relationships between banks, businesses, and government — what's often called crony capitalism. Once foreign investors got nervous about one country showing these cracks, they immediately suspected the same weaknesses existed next door, and pulled capital out of the whole region at once, not just the country where trouble started first.

Then the mechanism turned genuinely vicious. When a currency collapses, dollar-denominated debt doesn't shrink to match — it becomes dramatically more expensive to repay in local-currency terms. A currency crisis became a debt crisis within weeks, then a banking crisis as loans went bad, then a full-blown recession, all inside a matter of months.

The shock nobody priced in: South Korea

Thailand and Indonesia's troubles were unsettling; South Korea's were genuinely alarming to markets. Korea wasn't a fragile emerging economy by reputation — it had joined the OECD, the club of developed nations, in 1996. Watching a genuine industrial success story need emergency rescue made clear this wasn't a problem confined to obviously vulnerable economies.

The bailouts — and the road not taken

IMF-led rescue packages eventually totaled roughly $20 billion for Thailand, $40 billion for Indonesia, and $59 billion for South Korea — among the largest financial rescues assembled up to that point. They came with strict conditions: higher interest rates, tighter government spending, and financial-sector overhauls, terms many economists later argued deepened the immediate pain rather than easing it.

Malaysia took a different route entirely, and not right away. More than a year after the crisis began, on September 1, 1998, Prime Minister Mahathir Mohamad imposed capital controls — restricting money from flowing out of the country — against the advice of the IMF and even Malaysia's own central bank. It was treated as reckless at the time. Malaysia's subsequent recovery ended up comparable to the countries that had taken the IMF route, and the episode forced a real, ongoing reconsideration among economists of the blanket assumption that capital controls always make things worse.

Why India mostly watched from the sidelines: India had tightened its own capital account controls following its 1991 crisis, just six years earlier. That earlier, painful lesson left India comparatively insulated from the worst of 1997's contagion — one crisis's defensive scar tissue happening to pay off just in time for the next one, next door.

The lasting legacy shows up in central bank balance sheets across Asia today, the RBI's included: a shared determination to hold large foreign exchange reserves specifically as insurance against ever facing this kind of speculative vulnerability again — the same reserve-management instinct the RBI dispatch already described, and the same contagion pattern 1929 and 2008 both demonstrated in their own ways: a shared weakness, once exposed in one place, rarely stays contained to just that one place.
