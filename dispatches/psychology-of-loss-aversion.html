The Psychology of Loss Aversion

Most people would turn down a coin flip offering a 50% chance to lose ₹100 and a 50% chance to win ₹150 — even though, on average, it pays. That single refusal explains an enormous amount of bad investing behavior.

5 min read · Supplementary dispatch

Try the bet yourself, honestly: a coin flip, 50/50, where heads costs you ₹100 and tails pays you ₹150. Run it enough times and you come out ahead — the math is unambiguous. Most people still say no. Not because they've miscalculated the odds, but because losing ₹100 simply feels worse than winning ₹150 feels good, and that feeling wins the argument before the arithmetic gets a vote.

Where this was actually measured

In 1979, psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published "Prospect Theory" — now considered one of the most influential papers in the social sciences — laying out how people actually weigh gains and losses, as opposed to how classical economics assumed they would. Their central, repeatedly replicated finding: losses hurt roughly twice as much, psychologically, as an equivalent gain feels good. Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences largely for this work; Tversky had died in 1996 and the prize, by rule, isn't awarded posthumously.

What this does to real investors

Loss aversion doesn't stay confined to coin-flip experiments — it quietly reshapes how people actually manage money.

The disposition effect: investors tend to sell winning positions too early, to lock in the good feeling of a gain before it can slip away, while holding onto losing positions far too long, refusing to "realize" the loss in the hope it recovers first. It's precisely backwards from the discipline most experienced investors preach — cut losses, let winners run — and loss aversion is the reason it's so hard to actually do.

Panic-selling at the worst possible moment: both the 1929 crash and the 2008 crash describe forced, cascading selling — but plenty of that selling wasn't forced by margin calls at all. It was loss-averse investors watching a falling portfolio, feeling the mounting pain of an unrealized loss, and selling simply to make that feeling stop — converting a temporary paper loss into a permanent, realized one at exactly the moment prices were lowest.

Excessive caution in the other direction: the same bias can also keep people out of investing entirely, parking everything in cash or a savings account because the mere possibility of loss feels disproportionately threatening — even when the long-run math, from Module 1's inflation erosion to the Index Funds dispatch's decades of evidence, clearly favors participating.

The practical antidote

You don't out-argue a wired-in psychological bias with willpower alone. The more reliable fix is designing decisions so the bias never gets a vote in the moment. That's the entire quiet genius of a SIP, as the ₹500 Habit described: the buying decision is made once, in advance, on a calm day — not repeatedly, under the emotional pressure of a falling or euphoric market.

Feel it safely first: the Paper Trade simulator on the Practice Sheets page lets you experience exactly this pull — the urge to sell into a dip, the reluctance to sell a winner — with a fictional stock and zero real money on the line. It's worth noticing the impulse before it ever shows up with real stakes attached.

Loss aversion isn't a personal failing or a sign of weak discipline — it shows up in test subjects across cultures, income levels, and even species in related experiments. It's a feature of how human brains are built, not a bug in any one investor. The practical goal was never to feel differently about losses. It's to build a plan, in advance, that doesn't need you to.
