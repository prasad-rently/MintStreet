The First Wall Street Crash: 1929 and the Making of the Great Depression

Where it all started. The boom, the borrowed money, who got greedy, who profited, who lost everything — and the twenty-five years it took the market to recover. The founding proof that investing is risk, never a guarantee.

8 min read · Supplementary dispatch

Every dispatch on this site eventually has to reckon with one uncomfortable fact: markets can go down a lot, for a long time, and nobody rings a bell to warn you first. There's no cleaner place to see that fact in full than New York, autumn 1929 — the event every later crash on this site, from Lehman Brothers to the dot-com bust, is measured against.

The setup

The 1920s in America are remembered as the Roaring Twenties for a reason: rising industrial output, new consumer goods, and a stock market that seemed to only go up. That last part pulled in an enormous number of ordinary people who had never owned a share before — and the mechanism that let them in was margin buying. Under the loose rules of the time, a buyer could put down as little as 10% of a stock's price in cash and borrow the rest from a broker or bank. Put down $100, control $1,000 of stock. By August 1929, brokers had lent out more than $8.5 billion this way — a staggering sum for the era, almost all of it betting that prices would keep climbing.

That's the setup worth sitting with: leverage doesn't just amplify gains. It also means a fairly small price drop can wipe out your entire stake and leave you owing money on top of it — the same mechanic Module 4's glossary describes for margin and leverage today, just without modern safeguards to slow it down.

The crash

The Dow Jones Industrial Average had peaked at 381.17 on September 3, 1929. Cracks showed through October, and then came four days that earned their own names: Black Thursday (October 24), Black Monday (October 28, when the Dow fell nearly 13% in a single session), and Black Tuesday (October 29). Over that stretch the index fell roughly 25% — and that was only the beginning of a much longer slide.

The mechanism made it worse than a simple sell-off. Investors who'd bought on margin got margin calls — demands to put up more cash immediately or have their shares sold to cover the loan. Forced selling pushed prices down further, which triggered the next round of margin calls, which forced more selling. It was a feedback loop with no one in charge of stopping it.

Who got greedy

There's no single villain, which is part of the lesson. Millions of ordinary investors treated a rising market as a sure thing and borrowed to chase it further than their savings alone would have taken them. Banks and brokers lent freely into that speculation with little regard for whether it could be repaid if prices ever turned. And there was outright manipulation too: coordinated "pools" of wealthy insiders would quietly buy a stock together to drive its price up, draw in the public once the rally looked real, and sell out at the top — a much less regulated, much larger-scale cousin of the pump-and-dump playbook. Almost none of it was illegal yet. The rules that would later make most of it illegal didn't exist until after the crash proved they were needed.

Who came out ahead — and what happened to them after

A small number of people read the excess correctly and positioned against it. Joseph P. Kennedy — later JFK's father — is remembered for a story that's become a Wall Street parable: when a shoeshine boy started offering him stock tips, Kennedy reportedly decided that a market where shoeshine boys had opinions on stocks was a market with no one left to buy, sold out, and preserved his fortune.

The trader who profited most directly was Jesse Livermore, a famous speculator who bet heavily against the market in the days before the crash and by some accounts made roughly $100 million doing it — one of the largest trading profits in history at the time. It's tempting to end his story there. It's more honest not to: Livermore lost every dollar of that fortune and more in the market rallies of the early 1930s, was declared bankrupt, and took his own life in 1940. Even the person who "won" the crash didn't beat the market in any lasting sense — he just moved his losses a few years down the road.

Who lost everything

Ordinary margin investors were wiped out fastest and hardest — many didn't just lose their investment, they owed their broker money they no longer had. But the damage didn't stay confined to Wall Street. Bank runs and failed speculative loans on their books brought down more than 5,000 U.S. banks by 1933, taking ordinary depositors' savings with them — there was no deposit insurance yet to stop that. Industrial production collapsed, and unemployment in the United States reached roughly 25% by 1933. What started as a stock market crash became a decade-long global Great Depression, amplified further by countries raising trade tariffs and clinging to a gold standard that left central banks with little room to respond.

The recovery no one could have waited out

Here's the number worth sitting with longer than any other in this dispatch. The Dow bottomed at 41.22 on July 8, 1932 — 89% below its 1929 peak. It did not close above that 1929 high again until November 23, 1954. Twenty-five years. Someone who bought at the very top in 1929 and simply held on, doing everything "right" by today's buy-and-hold conventional wisdom, would have waited a full generation just to get back to even — before even accounting for inflation along the way.

This is the point of this dispatch: "the market always recovers eventually" is true in the sense that it's happened before. It is not the same as "the market recovers on a timeline you can afford to wait for." Those are different claims, and conflating them is one of the most common ways people talk themselves into risk they haven't actually thought through.

What changed

The reforms that followed read like a direct response to everything above. The Banking Act of 1933 (Glass-Steagall) separated ordinary commercial banking from the riskier business of investment banking, and created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) so a bank failure would no longer automatically mean depositors lost their savings. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) — a dedicated regulator with real power to police exactly the kind of manipulation and unchecked margin lending that had just proven so destructive. If that pattern — catastrophe first, statutory regulator with teeth second — sounds familiar, it should: it's the same shape India's own SEBI story would take more than sixty years later.

None of this is ancient history for the sake of it. It's the clearest possible illustration of something every other dispatch on this site takes as a starting assumption: a share of stock is not a savings account, a rising market is not evidence that it can't fall, and no amount of confidence — from a shoeshine boy, a famous trader, or anyone else — removes the fact that investing means accepting real risk in exchange for the chance of real reward. Everything from here builds on that.
