Insurance vs. Investment: Why LIC Policies Aren't SIPs

Insurance and investing solve two genuinely different problems. Bundling them into one policy — the way a classic "LIC policy" does — usually does both jobs worse than handling them separately.

6 min read · Supplementary dispatch

The Life Insurance Corporation of India was formed in 1956, when the government nationalized the entire life insurance industry into a single state-run entity. LIC became so dominant, for so long, that "LIC policy" turned into shorthand for "life insurance" in everyday conversation — the way "Xerox" came to mean any photocopy, regardless of which insurer or machine actually did the job. That total dominance is part of why so many Indians grew up thinking of insurance and investment as basically the same thing. They aren't.

What insurance actually is

Insurance is a risk-pooling mechanism, not a growth mechanism. A large number of people each pay a comparatively small premium into a shared pool. The unlucky few who suffer the insured event — in life insurance, dying during the policy term — receive a large payout from that pool. It exists to protect against one specific, low-probability, catastrophic event, not to make your money grow. Asking an insurance policy to also be a good investment is a bit like asking a fire extinguisher to also heat your home — it's not that it's impossible to combine, it's that each job gets compromised by the other.

Two very different products, one label

Term insurance is pure protection. Die during the term, and your family receives the full sum insured. Outlive the term, and you get nothing back — but because there's no savings component quietly riding along, the premium for a given amount of coverage is dramatically lower.

Endowment plans — the classic "LIC policy" in the popular imagination — bundle a much smaller amount of life coverage with a savings component, and promise a maturity payout even if you're alive and well when the term ends. It feels like getting something for nothing: protection and a payout. The bundling isn't actually free.

The math underneath the bundle

Because an endowment plan has to cover agent commissions, administrative costs, and a conservative guaranteed-return structure, the effective growth rate on its savings portion has historically run in the neighborhood of 4–5% a year — frequently below the inflation rate Module 1's Shrinking Rupee calculator already showed eating into rupee value over time. Worse, that same bundling means an endowment plan typically buys far less life coverage per rupee of premium than a term plan would for identical money.

The well-known alternative is usually summarized as "buy term, invest the rest": buy a large amount of pure term coverage cheaply, and separately put whatever you would have overpaid for an endowment plan's savings component into an actual investment vehicle — an index fund SIP, for instance. Run side by side over 15–20 years, independent comparisons have found this combination can build a meaningfully larger corpus than a traditional endowment plan — sometimes several times larger — while providing more life coverage throughout the whole period, not less.

Why the bundled version persists anyway

Two forces keep endowment-style policies popular despite the math. First, agents have historically earned meaningfully higher commissions selling traditional bundled plans than selling term plans — a real conflict of interest sitting inside the very recommendation you're receiving. Second, and more psychologically interesting: "getting nothing back" from a term plan you survive genuinely feels like a loss, even though you received exactly the product you paid for — protection during the years your family actually needed it. That's precisely the bias the companion dispatch on loss aversion describes, just wearing an insurance policy instead of a stock chart.

A more modern bundled product, the Unit Linked Insurance Plan (ULIP), links coverage to market-linked investment units rather than a fixed guaranteed rate. Regulatory reforms over the years have capped some of its charges, narrowing the efficiency gap — but the fundamental tension of asking one product to do two jobs at once hasn't disappeared, just softened.

None of this is an argument against having insurance — a family's primary earner absolutely needs adequate coverage, full stop. It's the same separation-of-concerns idea Module 2 already introduced for why a stock market exists alongside banks in the first place: protection and growth are two different jobs, built on two different kinds of math, and a single product trying to do both at once rarely does either one particularly well.
